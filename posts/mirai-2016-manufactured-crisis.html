Mirai, 2016: the internet's biggest botnet started as a Minecraft protection racket

November 3, 2026

On 21 October 2016, a botnet called Mirai hit the DNS provider Dyn with three waves of traffic and took down Twitter, Netflix, Spotify, Reddit, and a long list of other sites for most of a day. Commentators reached for the obvious framing: a nation-state had found a new way to break the internet, and the Internet of Things was now a weapon. The actual story is smaller and dumber than that. Mirai was built by three men in their late teens and early twenties to run a protection racket against Minecraft server hosts. The Dyn attack was a side effect of that racket getting away from them, not a demonstration of anyone's strategic intent.

Paras Jha, Josiah White, and Dalton Norman wrote Mirai to find and infect insecure IoT devices: security cameras, home routers, digital video recorders. The malware scanned the internet for devices still running one of about sixty default factory username-and-password pairs, logged in over Telnet, and added each one to the botnet. None of this took real skill. The devices were insecure because their manufacturers shipped them that way and never expected anyone to look.

The business model came first. Jha and White ran DDoS attacks against companies that hosted Minecraft servers, then offered those same companies a paid service to make the attacks stop. This is extortion with a technical layer on top, not a new category of cybercrime. Jha had already DDoSed his own university, Rutgers, during exam weeks and course registration, for reasons that look more like grudge than strategy; he was later ordered to pay Rutgers $8.6 million in restitution. Wikipedia's own framing for the pre-Dyn phase of Mirai is blunt: the authors used it to operate a protection racket against Minecraft servers.

The botnet outgrew the racket. On 20 September 2016, a Mirai-powered attack hit the security journalist Brian Krebs with 620 gigabits per second of traffic, the largest Krebs's own DDoS-mitigation provider, Akamai, had ever absorbed for a client. Akamai was providing that protection for free and dropped Krebs rather than let the attack degrade service for paying customers. Days later, whoever used the handle Anna-senpai posted Mirai's full source code to a hacking forum, which is a strange thing to do if you are trying to keep a weapon under your control. It looks much more like someone trying to put distance between themselves and a botnet that had already generated more law-enforcement attention than they wanted. Within weeks, copies of that code, run by other people, built the botnet that hit Dyn.

The men who built Mirai were not trying to take down Twitter. They were not trying to make a point about IoT security, or demonstrate a capability to a state sponsor, or do anything you could call strategic. They wanted Minecraft hosting customers to pay them to stop the pain. The attack that made every major news outlet treat IoT security as an urgent national conversation was generated by a side hustle that got bigger than its creators could steer.

This should change how you read every big attack that gets covered as evidence of growing cyber-threat sophistication. Scale of damage is not evidence of scale of intent. A piece of code built to bully a fourteen-year-old's game server into paying twenty dollars a month can, once released, take a continent's social media offline. The actual security failure here sat with device manufacturers who shipped hardcoded credentials for a decade and never corrected it even after Mirai made the exploit public and trivial. Default passwords are a known, fixable, boring problem, and boring problems don't get fixed by headlines about nation-state cyberwar; they get fixed by liability, and nobody made the manufacturers pay for this one.

Jha, White, and Norman eventually pleaded guilty in December 2017 to charges including violations of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, and separately to running a botnet-driven click-fraud scheme that paid them roughly 200 bitcoin, worth about $180,000 at the time. None of them were state actors, hacktivists, or even particularly competent criminals; Jha got caught in part because he kept attacking his own school. The next time a DDoS attack takes down infrastructure you rely on, the honest first question is not "who sent this message," it's "who's trying to get paid," and the honest answer, more often than the cyberwar framing admits, is some bored twenty-year-old running a protection racket that worked better than he expected.

Sources: Wikipedia's entries on Mirai (malware) and the DDoS attacks on Dyn; Brian Krebs, "Mirai IoT Botnet Co-Authors Plead Guilty," KrebsOnSecurity, December 2017; U.S. Department of Justice press releases on the Mirai and Rutgers University DDoS cases.
