Second Order

The 2010 flash crash got two official explanations. Only one blamed a real villain

November 11, 2026

On May 6, 2010, the Dow fell nearly 1,000 points in nine minutes and recovered most of it in the next twenty. Regulators needed an explanation fast. They produced one in five months. It named a culprit, a mechanism, and a lesson. Five years later they produced a second, incompatible explanation, and nobody went back to retract the first. A market event doesn't need a correct explanation to get treated as solved. It needs a confident one that arrives before people stop asking.

The first story came from a joint SEC and CFTC report in October 2010. A mutual fund, later named as Waddell & Reed, had sold 75,000 E-Mini S&P 500 futures contracts worth about $4.1 billion. Its algorithm sold based on volume alone and ignored price, so as the market thinned out it kept dumping contracts into a falling market instead of slowing down. Other automated systems saw the selling and pulled out, liquidity vanished, and the price went into freefall until a five-second circuit breaker paused trading long enough for buyers to come back in.

It read clean. One firm, one bad algorithm, one fixable flaw. The press ran with it, the SEC proposed new circuit breaker rules, and the story became the flash crash's official cause.

Then Nanex, a market data firm that had done its own tick-by-tick analysis, pointed out a problem nobody in the report addressed: Waddell & Reed could not trade E-Mini contracts directly. Only members of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange can, and Waddell & Reed wasn't one. The actual trade had been executed by its broker, Barclays Capital, using Barclays' own algorithm, which Nanex founder Eric Hunsader said showed clear evidence of accounting for price. The report never mentioned Barclays, never interviewed its engineers by the CFTC's own admission, and pinned the entire mechanism on an algorithm whose existence the report had the wrong owner for.

Waddell & Reed's only public response was a statement agreeing that "the events of May 6 involve multiple issues that transcend the actions of any single market participant." That's a company declining to say the regulators got it wrong while making clear it isn't accepting the story either.

Five years after the report, the Department of Justice filed a different, unrelated set of charges. Navinder Singh Sarao, a trader working from his parents' house in suburban London, had spent hours before the crash placing and cancelling tens of thousands of E-Mini sell orders he never intended to execute, a technique called spoofing, designed to fake pressure on the price. The CFTC's 2014 study of the crash concluded Sarao's orders were "at least significantly responsible" for the imbalance that preceded it. This is a second, separate causal claim about the same nine minutes, built on evidence the 2010 report either didn't have or didn't use, and it does not require the Waddell & Reed algorithm story to be true at all.

So the flash crash now has two official U.S. government explanations, filed four years apart, that don't agree on what caused it, and neither one has been withdrawn. The SEC's proposed fix, limit-up-limit-down trading halts, got built around the first explanation. It's still in place. Nobody revisited whether the regulation matches the cause once the cause changed.

Regulators investigating a market crash are not neutral historians. They are also the people who will be asked, in congressional testimony the following week, what happened and what they're doing about it. A specific, nameable failure mode is useful to them whether or not it's the most defensible one available at the time. "A fund's algorithm ignored price" is a sentence a senator can repeat. "We don't yet know, and two different mechanisms are both plausible" is not, and it also doesn't produce a rule anyone can point to afterward. The explanation that ships first is the one optimized for being repeatable, not the one optimized for being correct.

This isn't a conspiracy, and it doesn't mean spoofing was irrelevant. It means a nine-minute event with millions of order messages is genuinely hard to reconstruct, and the institutions responsible for explaining it had a closing date before they had a complete answer. The flash crash wasn't solved in October 2010. It was narrated in October 2010, and the narration outlived the evidence it was built on by half a decade before anyone noticed, or cared enough to fix the rule it produced.
