Solar Sunrise, 1998: the Pentagon spent a week sure Iraq was hacking it. Two California teenagers were

September 17, 2026

Attribution during a crisis is not an analytical process. It is a guess that borrows the language of analysis to sound like one. Solar Sunrise proves this cleanly, because the guess was wrong, the guessers were serious professionals with real access to evidence, and the actual answer was sitting in a California suburb the whole time.

In February 1998, someone broke into a string of unclassified Department of Defense networks. The FBI later counted at least eleven compromised systems at bases including Andrews, Kelly, and Lackland Air Force Base, plus Naval Station Pearl Harbor and a Marine base in Okinawa. The method was not clever. The intruders exploited a known, unpatched bug in the statd service on Solaris 2.4, planted a sniffer to harvest passwords, and came back later to collect what it caught. Deputy Secretary of Defense John Hamre still called it "the most organized and systematic attack the Pentagon has seen to date," because the number and sequencing of targets looked coordinated, and because of when it happened.

The timing was the problem. The US was moving roughly 2,000 Marines and a third aircraft carrier toward the Persian Gulf that same week, over Iraq's refusal to let UN weapons inspectors do their jobs. Investigators tracing the intrusions found a hop through Emirnet, an internet service provider in the United Arab Emirates and one of the only gateways into Iraq at the time. Add a large, systematic-looking intrusion campaign to a hop through one of the few doors into Baghdad, during the exact week troops were deploying, and you get a theory that assembles itself. Richard Clarke, the White House's counterterrorism coordinator at the time, put it plainly afterward: "For days, critical days, as we were trying to get forces to the Gulf, we didn't know who was doing it. We assumed, therefore, it was Iraq."

Notice what did the work in that sentence. Not evidence of Iraqi involvement. The absence of any other explanation, filled in by the explanation that fit the news cycle. The UAE hop was a fact about internet topology in 1998, when very few paths in or out of Iraq existed. It said nothing about who was sitting at the keyboard. It got treated as if it did, because a war was already the frame everyone was thinking in, and a piece of infrastructure that happened to sit near Iraq slotted neatly into that frame.

The actual answer took weeks to run down and involved no nation-state at all. The FBI traced the intrusions to two high school students in Cloverdale, California, using the handles Makaveli and Stimpy, who pled guilty once caught. They had been taking direction from an eighteen-year-old Israeli hacker who went by The Analyzer, real name Ehud Tenenbaum, arrested by Israeli police in March 1998 on evidence supplied by US authorities. Tenenbaum later said he wanted to show the systems were insecure, not to steal anything for a government. Attorney General Janet Reno announced the arrests as a message to "would-be computer hackers all over the world." No message was sent to whoever decided a UAE gateway was proof of Iraqi state action, because that decision was never wrong enough, on the record, to require correcting in public.

The interagency response was not lazy or understaffed. The FBI, CIA, DISA, and NSA all worked the case. CENTCOM activated a defensive plan it had tested in an exercise months earlier. None of that changes what the process actually did during the days that mattered: it produced an attribution to a specific hostile state, based on a single ambiguous network fact and a coincidence of timing, and that attribution reached the level of the Deputy Secretary of Defense and CIA director before two teenagers with a known, unpatched bug were found responsible. More process did not produce a more careful answer. It produced the same answer more people believed.

That is the actual finding, and it does not age well for anyone who thinks attribution has gotten more rigorous since 1998 because the tools have gotten better. The tools identify hops, timestamps, and exploited services. They do not identify intent, and intent is what a war decision needs. Solar Sunrise shows a system that had good technical visibility and still filled the intent gap with whatever story matched the geopolitical mood that week. Better forensics closes the technical gap. It does nothing about the part of attribution that is really just narrative-matching wearing a lab coat, and that part is the one that almost got acted on.
