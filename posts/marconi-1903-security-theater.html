Rats, rats, rats: the 1903 wireless hack that proved nothing changes when a security claim gets debunked

Third post

A security claim that nobody has tried to break is not a security claim. It is an advertisement. The first public demonstration of that fact happened in 1903, in a London lecture theatre, and the man who proved it paid no price at all for being right. Neither did the man who was proven wrong.

On 4 June 1903, Guglielmo Marconi was building a reputation as the man who had tamed wireless telegraphy. He had sent a signal across the Atlantic two years earlier. He was now selling something more specific than long-distance communication: privacy. In February 1903 he told the St James Gazette, "I can tune my instruments so that no other instrument that is not similarly tuned can tap my messages." That was the product. Not just range. Confidentiality.

To prove it, his colleague John Ambrose Fleming set up a public demonstration at the Royal Institution. Marconi would send a message by wireless from a station in Poldhu, Cornwall, roughly 300 miles away. Fleming would receive it live, in front of an audience, using an untuned receiver rigged to a projector lamp and a Morse printer. Minutes before Marconi's signal was due, the printer started tapping out a word, over and over: "Rats." Then it moved on to a limerick mocking Marconi for "diddling the public quite prettily," followed by a few lines of Shakespeare picked for the insult. Then it stopped, right before the real signal came through, and the demonstration went ahead as planned.

Fleming was furious and wrote to The Times calling it "scientific hooliganism." He didn't have to hunt for long. Four days later the culprit wrote back, gladly signing his name: Nevil Maskelyne, a stage magician and wireless tinkerer who had been quietly employed by the Eastern Telegraph Company, a firm whose entire cable business Marconi's wireless technology threatened to make obsolete. Maskelyne had spent the previous year building a receiving mast on the Cornish coast and had already shown, in a trade journal, that he could pick up Marconi's supposedly tuned ship-to-shore traffic on ordinary untuned equipment. The Royal Institution stunt wasn't the discovery. It was the publicity tour for a discovery he'd already made and published, aimed at the one audience that mattered: the public Marconi had made his privacy claim to, in the room where he was making it loudest.

Marconi's tuning was real. It reduced interference. It was nowhere near selective enough to keep out a transmitter close by and moderately powerful, and it did nothing to stop an untuned receiver from picking up a tuned signal in the first place. Anyone building a broadband receiver, which is a much simpler and cheaper thing to build than a tuned one, could listen to everything. Fleming's own receiver in the theatre was untuned, for the mundane reason that a properly selective one would have been too bulky to demonstrate. Marconi's security model, as advertised, required equipment nobody involved was actually using.

Now the part that should bother you more than the hack itself. It changed nothing. Marconi didn't retract the claim, fix the underlying tuning problem in any way that mattered commercially, or lose customers. He opened the first commercial transatlantic wireless service in 1907. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1909, for contributions to wireless telegraphy, full stop, no asterisk about the privacy claim that a magician had shown to be false six years earlier in front of a paying audience and a national newspaper. Fleming, the man whose demonstration had just been publicly gutted, remained Marconi's paid technical advisor. The market had already decided what it wanted from Marconi, and privacy was a marketing line, not a load-bearing requirement. Nobody who mattered to his business asked him to prove it again.

That is the pattern, not the anecdote. A vendor makes a security claim. Someone with a motive to check, not a random hobbyist but an interested party with money on the outcome, actually tests it and finds it's false. The finding gets attention for a news cycle. The vendor's business is completely unaffected, because the customers were never buying the security property in the first place. They were buying the range, the speed, the convenience, the thing that actually showed up in daily use, and the security claim was a line item nobody had the tools or the incentive to verify for themselves. Maskelyne could check because he had a mast, a receiver, and an employer paying him specifically to find the hole. Marconi's actual customers, shipping companies and newspapers paying for wireless dispatches, had none of those three things.

Run that forward. "Military-grade encryption" on a consumer app. "Bank-level security" on a password manager. "Zero-knowledge" architecture that turns out to have a server-side key after all. Almost none of it gets tested by the people relying on it, because testing it requires exactly what Maskelyne had and ordinary customers don't: the equipment, the expertise, and a reason to look. It gets tested, when it gets tested at all, by a competitor, a researcher chasing a paper, or an attacker with something to gain. The claim survives right up until someone with the right incentive bothers to check, and it survives the checking too, more often than not, because being wrong in public has never reliably cost a vendor the sale.

Maskelyne was right. He proved it cleanly, with witnesses, and published his method so nobody could call it a fluke. It made no difference to who won. If you want the actual lesson from 1903, it isn't that Marconi's system had a hole. Every system has a hole. It's that being publicly, provably wrong about a security claim and continuing to profit from that claim are not in tension. They never have been.
