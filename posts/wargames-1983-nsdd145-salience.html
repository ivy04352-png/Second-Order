A RAND report warned about this in 1970. It took a movie to make anyone act

September 23, 2026

In February 1970 a Defense Science Board task force led by RAND's Willis Ware published a report called Security Controls for Computer Systems. It warned that shared computer systems, the kind that let multiple users and programs touch the same machine, had no reliable way to keep one user's access from bleeding into another's. The report was correct, specific, and largely ignored. Thirteen years later a Hollywood screenwriter interviewed Ware while researching a film about a teenager who hacks a military supercomputer and nearly starts a nuclear war. That film did what the report could not. It got a federal computer crime law passed, a presidential directive signed, and NSA authority expanded over civilian government networks, all within about eighteen months of release. The report described the actual risk. The movie is what moved the government. That gap is the finding, and it says the thing risk assessments never want to admit: institutions don't act on evidence, they act on a story they can picture.

The film was WarGames, released in June 1983. President Reagan watched it at Camp David the weekend it opened. Days later he was in a White House meeting with the secretaries of state, defense, and treasury, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, and sixteen members of Congress, ostensibly to discuss a new missile and arms talks with the Soviets. Reagan interrupted to describe the plot of the movie and asked General John Vessey, the Joint Chiefs chairman, "Could something like this really happen? Could someone break into our most sensitive computers?" A week later Vessey came back with an answer: "Mr. President, the problem is much worse than you think." Vessey wasn't guessing. The vulnerability had already been documented for over a decade. What changed was that the president had a scene in his head to attach it to.

The paper trail after that is not subtle. The House Committee Report backing the Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1984, the law that became the basis for the modern Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, includes a statement from a Telenet representative describing WarGames as "a realistic representation of the automatic dialing and access capabilities of the personal computer." A movie plot was entered into the legislative record as evidence for a criminal statute. Congress didn't do this because nobody had raised the underlying risk before. Computer intrusion had been a known and studied problem since the 1960s. Congress did it because a fictional teenager with a modem had made the risk legible to people who don't read RAND reports.

On September 17, 1984, Reagan signed National Security Decision Directive 145, the first presidential directive on computer security. It didn't just tell agencies to be careful. It handed the National Security Agency authority to set safeguards for "sensitive but unclassified" information across the civilian government, taking that job away from the National Bureau of Standards, which had held it under the 1965 Brooks Act. That's a huge jurisdictional transfer, done by directive rather than statute, driven by a scenario where the threat was an external hacker breaking into a defense supercomputer to nearly start a war. Real computer security problems in 1984 were mostly about weak access controls, sloppy operating procedures, and unencrypted data sitting on ordinary agency systems, not Cold War brinksmanship. NSDD-145 was shaped like the movie's threat, not like the actual one, because the movie's threat was the one that had gotten a directive written in the first place.

The mismatch showed up fast. Scientific organizations, civil liberties groups, and businesses objected to an intelligence agency with no public accountability setting security standards for civilian data, especially after an October 1986 NSC policy statement tried to define what counted as sensitive so broadly that it triggered public alarm and got rescinded within months. Congress spent 1987 unwinding NSDD-145 through hearings, and the Computer Security Act of 1987, signed into law that January, put standards authority back with NBS and reduced NSA to a technical advisor. A directive built to answer a movie's version of the threat took three years of public fighting to correct once real-world agencies had to live with it.

None of this means the underlying concern was wrong. Ware's report was right, computer intrusion was a real and growing problem, and something like the Computer Security Act was probably overdue. The point is narrower and less comfortable: the thing that got policy moving wasn't the correct, thirteen-year-old technical assessment sitting in a government file. It was a two-hour film that gave a specific person a picture to react to. When policy gets written from a picture instead of from a risk distribution, it inherits the picture's shape, right down to which agency gets the power and which threat gets funded. If you want to know why a given piece of security policy looks strangely mismatched to the risk it claims to address, don't ask what evidence produced it. Ask what story did.
