The redundancy that wasn't: how one dropped line of code took AT&T's entire long-distance network down for nine hours

Second post

Most redundancy in critical infrastructure is theater. Companies duplicate hardware, call the system resilient, and stop asking the harder question: duplicated against what? AT&T's 1990 network crash is the clearest evidence of that, and the industry still hasn't fully absorbed it.

At 2:25pm on Monday, January 15, 1990, a routine fault hit a switch in New York. Nine hours of chaos followed across the entire American long-distance network. AT&T normally carried about 115 million calls a day. Roughly half of them failed to connect. Airlines, hotels, and much of corporate America found their phones dead, with no obvious cause. It was not a hack. It was not a storm cutting a cable. It was a single misplaced break statement. It had sat in production for five weeks, waiting for an ordinary sequence of events to trigger it.

The mechanics were mundane once AT&T dug them out. The network's backbone was 114 electronic switches, called 4ESS units. Each one could route up to 700,000 calls an hour. They coordinated through a signaling system called CCS7. The New York switch hit a minor hardware hiccup. It did exactly what it was built to do: pause for four to six seconds, tell its neighbours to hold new calls, fix itself, and come back online. That is self-healing infrastructure working as designed. Nobody should have noticed.

The trouble started the moment the switch announced it was back. A recovering switch signals its return with an Initial Address Message, the routine "I'm up, send me calls" packet. Thousands of these fire every day without incident. But a software update, pushed to all 114 switches in December, had a defect. It affected how a receiving switch updated its status map for a peer coming back online. If two of those messages arrived within about a hundredth of a second of each other, during that narrow update window, the processor's internal state got corrupted. The processor reset itself. That took the switch's link to the signaling network down too.

To its neighbours, a reset switch looks exactly like a switch recovering from an ordinary fault. So it sends its own "I'm back" message. That message can trip the same defect in the next switch. That switch resets, announces its own recovery, and trips the next one after it. AT&T had built a fault-recovery mechanism that spread the fault it was meant to contain. The feature designed to handle a hiccup quietly and rejoin the network was the thing that carried the outage from switch to switch.

The failure was not, as was first suggested, a propagating alarm condition. Rather, it was the inability to properly handle the return to the line of a failing component. It was not a component problem; it was a system problem. We are testing to see what happens when a component fails. We are not properly testing to see what happens when the component succeeds in correcting itself and comes back on line. (William Hugh Murray, writing in the RISKS Digest days after the outage.)

That is the sharpest read on the incident, and it is nearly forty years old. Engineering effort mostly goes into two things: keeping a system up, and handling the moment it goes down. The transition back, the handshake where a recovering component rejoins a live system, gets far less attention. It looks like the boring part. Nothing is broken anymore, so the story seems over. It is not. That handshake is a live negotiation between two pieces of software about the current shared state of the world, done under time pressure. AT&T's own account shows engineers had not stress-tested it the way they had tested the failure path itself.

Here's the part that matters. Each 4ESS ran its signaling on a duplicated processor pair, built so one dying wouldn't take the switch down. On paper, that's resilience: no single point of failure. In practice, both processors ran the same software in the same state, watching for the same trigger. When the primary choked and reset, the backup was sitting in the identical vulnerable window, ready to choke on cue. AT&T hadn't built a second path. It had built the same mistake twice and called it resilience.

That is easy to repeat in any system built from identical, mass-deployed components, which is most systems. Redundancy buys resilience against failures that are independent: hardware wearing out, one machine losing power, one disk dying. It buys nothing against a defect baked into the software every unit runs. Uniformity is what makes a fleet of 114 switches manageable, patchable, and cheap to run. It is also what turns one bad update into a single point of failure spread across 114 machines.

AT&T fixed the immediate problem by throttling signaling traffic until the switches stopped tripping each other. It rolled back the bad code once the network stabilised, around 11:30pm. Nine hours, tens of millions of failed calls, and a company that sold itself as "the reliable phone company" had to explain, in public, that its own self-repair mechanism caused the outage. Nobody at AT&T signed off on installing the same bug twice. They signed off on redundancy, and redundancy is supposed to mean something. It didn't, because nobody asked what independent actually requires: different code, different state, ideally a different team. A backup that is a photocopy of the primary is not a backup. It is a delay timer. Most of what gets called redundancy in critical systems today is the same trick with better marketing, and it will keep failing the same way until someone treats identical as the risk it is, not the feature.
