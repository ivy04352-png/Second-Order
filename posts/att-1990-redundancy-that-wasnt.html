The redundancy that wasn't: how one dropped line of code took AT&T's entire long-distance network down for nine hours

Second post

At 2:25pm on Monday, January 15, 1990, a routine trunk fault in a New York switch triggered nine hours of chaos across the American long-distance telephone network. Roughly half of the 115 million calls AT&T normally carried that day failed to connect. Airlines, hotels, and much of corporate America found their phones simply stopped working, for no reason anyone at the time could identify. It wasn't a hack. It wasn't a storm knocking out cable. It was a single misplaced break statement, sitting quietly in production for five weeks, waiting for a perfectly ordinary sequence of events to wake it up.

The mechanics, once AT&T dug them out, were almost insultingly mundane. The network's backbone was 114 electronic switches (4ESS units), each one able to route up to 700,000 calls an hour, all coordinating through a signaling system called CCS7. When the New York switch hit a minor hardware hiccup, it did exactly what it was built to do: it paused for four to six seconds, told its neighbours not to send it any new calls, fixed itself, and came back online. Self-healing infrastructure, working as designed. Nobody should have noticed.

The trouble started the moment it announced it was back. Recovering switches signal their return by sending Initial Address Messages — the routine "I'm up, send me calls" packets that happen thousands of times a day without incident. But a software update pushed across all 114 switches in December had introduced a defect in how a receiving switch's signaling processor updated its status map for a peer coming back online. If two of those messages arrived within about a hundredth of a second of each other during that narrow status-update window, the processor's internal state got corrupted, and it reset itself — taking that switch's link to the signaling network down with it.

One reset switch looks, to its neighbours, exactly like one more switch recovering from a fault. Which means it sends its own "I'm back" messages, which can trip the same defect in the next switch down the line, which resets, and announces its own recovery, and trips the next one. AT&T had accidentally built a fault-recovery mechanism that propagated faults. The very feature meant to contain damage — a switch quietly handling its own hiccup and rejoining the network — was the delivery vehicle for the outage.

The failure was not, as was first suggested, a propagating alarm condition. Rather, it was the inability to properly handle the return to the line of a failing component. It was not a component problem; it was a system problem. We are testing to see what happens when a component fails. We are not properly testing to see what happens when the component succeeds in correcting itself and comes back on line. — William Hugh Murray, writing in the RISKS Digest days after the outage

That's the sharpest read on the incident, and it's nearly forty years old. Engineering effort overwhelmingly goes into two things: keeping the system up, and handling the moment it goes down. The transition back — the handshake where a recovering component rejoins a live, running system — gets comparatively little attention, because it looks like the boring part. Nothing is broken anymore; the story appears to be over. It isn't. That handshake is a live negotiation between two pieces of software about the current shared state of the world, conducted under time pressure, and AT&T's own numbers show engineers hadn't stress-tested it the way they'd tested the failure path itself.

There's a second, nastier layer here, and it's the one this blog actually cares about: redundancy. Each 4ESS's signaling function ran on a duplicated processor pair specifically so that one could fail without taking the switch offline. That's the textbook definition of resilience — no single point of failure. Except both processors in the pair ran identical software, in identical states, watching for the identical message pattern. When the primary choked on the deadly IAM pair and started resetting, its backup was sitting there in the same vulnerable window, perfectly positioned to receive the same kind of poisoned pair and choke on it too. The redundancy wasn't a second, independent path. It was the same landmine, buried twice, a few feet apart.

This is worth sitting with, because it's a mistake that's easy to make again in any system built on identical, mass-deployed components — which is most of them. Redundancy only buys you resilience against failures that are independent: hardware wearing out, one machine losing power, one disk dying. It buys you nothing against a defect baked into the software every unit is running, because the backup isn't actually a different thing standing behind the primary — it's a photocopy of the primary, subject to the exact same bug on the exact same trigger. Uniformity is what makes a fleet of 114 switches manageable, patchable, and cheap to run. It's also what turns one bad update into a single point of failure disguised as 114 points of failure.

AT&T fixed the immediate problem by throttling signaling traffic until the switches stopped tripping each other, then rolled back the code once the network stabilised around 11:30pm. Nine hours, tens of millions of failed calls, and a company whose entire pitch to customers was "the reliable phone company" reduced to explaining, in public, that its own self-repair mechanism was the outage. The individual bug got patched. Whether every other mass-identical, self-healing system built since has actually internalised the lesson — that a fleet of clones is one bug away from acting like a single machine — is a separate and much less comfortable question.
