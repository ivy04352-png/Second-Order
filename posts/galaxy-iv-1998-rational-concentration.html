One satellite carried ninety percent of America's pagers. Putting them all on it was the rational choice

September 24, 2026

On the afternoon of May 19, 1998, PanAmSat's Galaxy IV satellite began an uncontrolled spin. It sat 22,300 miles above Kansas. It stopped receiving and relaying signals. Within an hour, about ninety percent of the country's forty to forty-five million pagers went silent. Doctors, hospitals, stockbrokers, and gas station credit-card readers lost service at the same moment. The obvious reaction is to call this reckless. A whole country ran urgent communications through one satellite. That reaction is wrong. Every company that routed traffic through Galaxy IV made a sound economic choice. The outage does not disprove that. It shows what "sound" means once you remove hindsight.

The failure came from a known defect in the hardware. Hughes had used tin-plated solder on the satellite's control processors. These processors keep a satellite pointed at Earth. Under certain conditions, tin grows thin metal filaments called whiskers. A whisker can bridge two contacts and short a circuit. Galaxy IV's primary control processor failed this way. Its backup was supposed to take over automatically. It could not. The backup had already failed the same way, silently, about a year earlier. Nobody had a reason to test a backup system that had never been called on to work. PanAmSat's engineers later said they were not fully certain tin whiskers caused the second failure at all. Some space weather researchers blamed a run of solar storms two weeks earlier instead. The company never settled the question.

The part usually left out of the story is why so many companies ended up on the same satellite. Nearly every major paging company in the country used Galaxy IV. PageNet, SkyTel, PageMart, AirTouch, Arch, and MetroCall all sent traffic through it. They chose it independently, and for the same reason. It had strong transmission power. It also sat in an orbital slot over Kansas. Together those gave the widest, cleanest coverage of the continental United States that any satellite could offer. None of these companies were careless and none were colluding. Each one picked the best satellite for its own paying customers. That satellite turned out to be the same one every competitor had already picked. The concentration was not an oversight. It was the shared output of several companies independently doing their jobs well.

PanAmSat's president, Fred Landman, said the quiet part out loud after the fact. "This has never happened before in thirty-five years," he told reporters. "When things never happen, redundancy and backup capacity is not the first and foremost concern." He was not excusing negligence. He was describing a return-on-investment calculation that any competitor would have run the same way. Building standby satellite capacity against a failure with no precedent in thirty-five years is not caution. It is spending money against a risk nobody can price. The paging industry skipped that spending for the same reason most industries skip insurance against events with no track record. The expected cost of protection is higher than the expected cost of the risk, until the one time it is not.

The response, once the failure happened, was also market-driven rather than mandated. PanAmSat moved paging traffic to a nearby satellite, Galaxy 3-R, within a day. It got emergency approval from the FCC to drift its Galaxy VI satellite into the dead satellite's orbital slot, a six-day maneuver. Paging companies added backup satellite arrangements afterward, at their own expense, once the event had a track record of one. No regulator forced the industry to diversify before 1998. None forced a different approach afterward, beyond what companies chose to buy for themselves.

Stories like this usually end with a call to invest more in redundancy. That call is comforting and wrong. Redundancy against a rare, high-impact, shared failure is expensive to build. Whoever pays for it alone cannot capture the benefit, because every competitor keeps riding the same free convergence until the day it breaks. That describes the internet today. Most of its traffic still runs through a handful of cloud and content providers, picked for the same competitive reasons Galaxy IV was. Nobody with a normal balance sheet is going to unilaterally build redundancy against an event with no track record. Companies will keep making the correct call. They will keep making it right up until it stops being correct.
