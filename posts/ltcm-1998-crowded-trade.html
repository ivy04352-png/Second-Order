Second Order

LTCM, 1998: the models were right. The room was the problem

November 17, 2026

Long-Term Capital Management gets filed as a story about arrogant quants and a model that broke. That's wrong. The model's core bet, that prices for near-identical assets converge over time, was correct almost every time LTCM made it. The fund failed because it never priced in how many other desks were making the exact same bet with the exact same money behind it. Diversification doesn't protect you from a crowd. It just hides the crowd from your spreadsheet.

LTCM opened in 1994, run by former Salomon Brothers bond chief John Meriwether and staffed with some of the sharpest people in finance, including two future Nobel laureates in economics, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton. Their strategy was relative-value arbitrage: find two securities that should trade at nearly the same price, bet on the gap closing, and size the bet so the tiny gap turns into a real return once you lever it up enough. The fund returned 20 percent in 1994, 43 percent in 1995, 41 percent in 1996, and 17 percent in 1997. Wall Street treated LTCM's partners like oracles.

The leverage is the part everyone remembers, and it was extreme. By the end of 1997, LTCM held about $30 of debt for every $1 of its own capital. Even that number understates the real exposure, because the fund had returned capital to investors that same year without shrinking its positions, raising its effective leverage further while its asset base shrank. But leverage alone didn't kill it. Leverage just means your gains and losses arrive faster. What killed it was being levered on a trade that half of Wall Street was also running.

LTCM wasn't the only firm doing convergence arbitrage. The proprietary trading desks at the same banks who were LTCM's counterparties and investors were running similar positions, because the trades looked obviously profitable to anyone with the same models and the same data. When the 1997 Asian crisis bled into the 1998 Russian default, investors everywhere ran for the most liquid, safest instruments they could find and dumped everything else. That's a flight to quality, and it doesn't care whether your spread is backed by sound math. Prices that were supposed to converge diverged further, because everyone holding the same positions was trying to sell at once. One LTCM partner, Victor Haghani, described it afterward: it was as if someone else held their exact portfolio, three times the size, and was liquidating all of it simultaneously. He wasn't speculating. He was describing the market.

By August 1998 the fund had lost $1.85 billion. By the third week of September, its equity had fallen from $2.3 billion to $400 million. The firm wasn't failing because its thesis on any single trade was wrong. The Royal Dutch/Shell pairs trade, one of the classic LTCM positions, eventually converged the way the fund expected. It just didn't converge before the fund was forced to unwind it at a loss, because LTCM had run out of capital to hold the position open. Being right eventually and being solvent in the meantime are different problems, and leverage is what turns the gap between them into a bankruptcy.

The rescue is usually told as a clean save: fourteen banks put up $3.625 billion, the Federal Reserve coordinated the room, no taxpayer money changed hands. All true, and it's the detail the Fed's own retrospective leans on hardest, because it lets everyone call this a private-sector solution with no moral hazard. That framing skips the actual reason the Fed got involved at all. The Fed didn't organize a rescue because it liked LTCM's partners. It organized one because LTCM's counterparties were also its lenders, its trading partners, and in some cases its investors, and an uncontrolled LTCM failure would have forced every one of them to dump the same illiquid positions into a market that had no buyers left. The risk wasn't LTCM. The risk was that an entire tier of Wall Street had built overlapping exposure to the same bets without anyone keeping a master list of who held what.

Call that what it is: concentration risk wearing a diversification costume. LTCM's own book was spread across hundreds of positions in bonds, equities, mortgages, and derivatives, which is exactly the kind of spread that is supposed to mean safety. It didn't, because the relevant variable wasn't how many different trades the fund held. It was how many other players, inside and outside the fund, were exposed to the same underlying factor: the price of liquidity itself, moving against everyone at once. No amount of position-level diversification fixes a single shared risk factor nobody is tracking across firms.

Nobody has fixed that. Banks still run prime brokerage books with no central view of what their biggest hedge fund clients hold at other banks, which is precisely how Archegos blew up in 2021, twenty-three years after LTCM and for close to the same reason. The models get smarter. The spreadsheets get prettier. Nobody still has the master list.
