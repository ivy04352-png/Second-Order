How systems behave under pressure, versus how they say they behave.

  • The Cuckoo's Egg wasn't a story about persistence. It was proof nobody owned the problem

    September 21, 2026 · English / 中文

    In 1986 a Berkeley sysadmin chased a 75-cent accounting error and found a KGB-linked spy ring inside US military networks. The FBI, CIA, and NSA all turned him down first, because none of them had a category for the crime. That gap in ownership, not Stoll's persistence, is the real finding, and it still exists.

  • Solar Sunrise, 1998: the Pentagon spent a week sure Iraq was hacking it. Two California teenagers were

    September 17, 2026 · English / 中文

    In February 1998 investigators traced a wave of Pentagon intrusions through one of the only internet gateways into Iraq, days before a planned strike, and concluded Iraq was behind it. The intruders were two high school students in California taking orders from an Israeli teenager. Attribution under pressure isn't analysis. It's a guess dressed up as one.

  • Stanislav Petrov didn't save the world. The story we tell about him hid a bigger failure

    September 15, 2026 · English / 中文

    In 1983 a Soviet officer ignored a false missile warning and the story became one man's good judgment saving the world. The real finding was that the Soviet military had fielded a defective early-warning system, and rewarding the man instead of fixing the program was cheaper than admitting it.

  • No alarm is not the same as all clear: what the 2003 blackout proves about silent failure

    September 14, 2026 · English / 中文

    FirstEnergy's control room ignored a warning phone call because their monitoring screen showed nothing wrong. The screen showed nothing wrong because the alarm system had silently frozen forty minutes earlier. The largest blackout in North American history came from treating silence as confirmation instead of as a missing answer.

  • Known, admitted, and left in anyway: what the 1988 Morris worm actually proves about patching

    September 9, 2026 · English / 中文

    The bug that took down 10% of the early internet in 1988 wasn't hidden. Sendmail's own author had built the backdoor himself, knew it was still reachable, and left it in because it was useful. People don't fix known holes because they're known. They fix them when the bill finally arrives.

  • Rats, rats, rats: the 1903 wireless hack that proved nothing changes when a security claim gets debunked

    September 4, 2026 · English / 中文

    In 1903 a magician hired by a rival telegraph company hacked Marconi's "unbreakable" wireless system live, in front of an audience, and proved the privacy claim was false. Marconi kept selling it anyway, won the Nobel Prize six years later, and lost no customers. Being publicly wrong about a security claim and profiting from it have never been in tension.

  • The redundancy that wasn't: how one dropped line of code took AT&T's entire long-distance network down for nine hours

    September 3, 2026 · English / 中文

    Most redundancy in critical infrastructure is theater. AT&T built a backup processor so a failure in one wouldn't take a switch offline, but the backup ran the identical buggy software in the identical vulnerable state. A single misplaced line of code cascaded across 114 switches and killed half the nation's long-distance calls for nine hours.

  • One customer, one flag, half the internet: what Fastly's 2021 outage actually shows about "resilient" systems

    August 20, 2026

    A single valid, ordinary configuration change from one customer took down a meaningful slice of the web in 2021. Nobody did anything wrong. A dormant bug had been sitting in production for weeks, waiting for the right input, which is exactly why "nobody did anything wrong" is not the reassurance it sounds like.
